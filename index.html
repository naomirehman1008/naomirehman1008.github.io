About Me

Hello! I'm Naomi, a computer architect passionate about making things go fast for less. Architecture doesn't exist in isolation, so I like to consider challenges and opportunities across both hardware and applications. My work ranges anywhere from writing Verilog to working in vLLM. My research currently focuses on making language models more efficient from both the architecture and algorithmic side. I'm pursuing my PhD at UC Santa Barbara in the ArchLab, co-advised by Tim Sherwood and Jonathan Balkind.


Before coming to Santa Barbara I did my undergrad in Computer Science and Computer Engineering at UCSC, where I worked with Professor Heiner Litz on CPU frontend architecture and Professor Tyler Sorensen on GPU programming model specifications. I've interned several times on NVIDIA's GPU Architecture team, where I've worked on various projects including functional testing and feature design. In my free time, I enjoy reading and baking.


I'm grateful to the NSF Graduate Research Fellowship Program (GRFP) and UCSB's CS department for the fellowships that fund my research.

Research Projects

Efficient Fine-Tuning on Edge Devices

Advised by Profs. Tim Sherwood and Jon Balkind

While edge devices are increasingly incorporating small, quantized neural processors for inference, they lack the higher precision hardware needed for training. At the same time, these devices hold rich user data that could personalize models, but sending that data to the cloud for training raises privacy concerns. Zeroth Order training has been shown to successfully train even at low precisions, however, in practice it requires significantly more training steps and thus more energy than backpropagation. In this work, we aim to close the energy gap between backpropagation and zeroth order training through hardware-software co-design, enabling user specialization through fine-tuning on their devices.

Memory Efficient Language Models

In Collaboration with Prof. Xifeng Yan

While serving large language models, their KV tensors are cached on device rather than recomputed each step. The KV cache consumes a significant portion of VRAM, limiting the maximum achievable batch size. In this work, we significantly reduce the KV cache footprint through a more efficient model architecture, increasing the batch size and serving throughput.

GPU Subgroup Semantics (SIMT-Step)

Advised by Prof. Tyler Sorensen

Subgroups (i.e. warps in CUDA) are logical groups of threads in the GPU execution hierarchy. While it is informally recognized that they execute in lockstep, specification designers are hesitant to formally say this in the programming model, leaving the semantics of subgroup execution, notably collective subgroup operations and memory operations, underdefined. This work formalizes several potential subgroup execution models and evaluate their support on existing systems. We recently published this work in PLDI!

Incorrect Path Prediction

Advised by Prof. Heiner Litz

Datacenter CPUs have very deep and wide pipelines. They rely on branch predictions to overcome control hazards and keep the pipeline full. While modern branch predictors are highly accurate, bad speculation is still an issue, especially for larger applications. This project aims to predict when the frontend is producing erroneous instructions and leverage this prediction for a variety of efficiency and performance optimizations. My undergraduate thesis on this topic, Incorrect Path Prediciton, won the Steck Family award, given annually to the single best Senior thesis at UCSC and UCSC's highest undergraduate honor.
